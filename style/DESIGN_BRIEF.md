# Design Brief: A Written Mood Board

**LINEAGE.** This descends from the paper bureaucracy of American meteorology — the climatological data sheets that regional weather bureau offices typed, stamped, and filed in the decades after the war, when weather was a civic utility run by men in short sleeves who smoked at their desks. Its ancestors are the government form, the surplus-store field manual, the teletype bulletin, and the graph-paper plot drafted by hand and photocopied until the lines went soft. It shares blood with mission-control consoles and airport operations rooms, but its true parent is humbler: the filing cabinet. This aesthetic was native to a world where data was scarce, arrived once a day, and was recorded because recording it was somebody's job. The interface carries that world's assumption forward — that a weather station is an institution, that a station identifier is a proper name worth printing in the masthead, and that thirty years of daily observations is a civic monument, not a "dataset."

**EMOTIONAL REGISTER.** Sitting in front of this should feel like being handed the station logbook by a clerk who trusts you to read it. It is warm the way an archive is warm — not friendly, but hospitable to the patient. There is no seduction and no onboarding; the numbers are simply *there*, dense and level, and the interface's posture says: you came here because you wanted the record, and here is the record. It treats the user as a fellow record-keeper, someone who knows what a record high means and wants the date it happened. The pleasure it offers is the pleasure of ritual consultation — the almanac checked over coffee, the same page returned to on the same day each year. It should feel slightly official and slightly loved, like a public document someone has clearly been maintaining with private devotion.

**MATERIALITY.** If you could pick it up, it would be a manila folder of typed reports on cream stock, warmed and yellowed at the edges, with a carbon copy underneath — every element sits on the page with the flat, doubled shadow of stacked paper, never the soft glow of glass. The palette is the palette of the object itself: aged pulp, faded typewriter ribbon, the dull orange of a safety placard, the drab olive of government-issue anything. Texture is everywhere but quiet — the tooth of graph paper, the faint dot of halftone, the diagonal hatch where an observation is missing (absence, too, gets an official notation). Everything is squared, ruled, and edged; corners are cut, not rounded. Space is rationed the way it is on a form: dense where the data lives, generous only in the margins where a stamp might land. And it is quiet — nothing slides, nothing fades, nothing eases. When the machine is thinking, it says so in blinking capitals, like a terminal, and when it is done, the answer simply appears, the way a page is turned rather than dissolved.

**PERSONALITY & VOICE.** The rules here are stern — no softness, no motion, no ornament — which is exactly what makes the small violations sing. The indulgences are clerical, not decorative: a circular stamp sitting askew in the corner at partial ink, as if the clerk pressed it in a hurry; a little amber note slapped on at a slight angle telling you the chart can be dragged, like a strip of masking tape with instructions penciled on it. These are the only crooked things in a ruled world, and they are the wink — evidence of the human hand inside the institution. The voice is terse, upper-case, and telegraphic, closer to a station bulletin than a website; it says what the thing is and stops. What it refuses is the entire modern courtship: no hero image, no rounded pill buttons, no gradient mesh, no delighter animations, no emoji standing in for words, no pretense that the visitor needs to be convinced of anything. It will not perform enthusiasm. Its enthusiasm is the thirty-five years of daily readings it lays in front of you without comment.

**FAILURE MODES.** The lazy interpretation is nostalgia cosplay: cranking the sepia, distressing the edges, adding coffee stains and "vintage" grunge overlays until the thing looks like a prop from an escape room. That is embalming, not lineage — this design is a *living* document that happens to keep old habits, not a forgery of an old one. Equally wrong is drifting into adjacent retro-futures: the neon grid and chrome sunset of vaporwave, the beige-CRT kitsch of cassette-futurism, the twee symmetry of a boutique-hotel pastiche where everything is centered and precious. This aesthetic is none of those, because those are all about how the past *looks*, and this is about how the past *worked*. It is also not web-brutalism's aggressive ugliness — the rulings and stamps here come from care, not contempt. The test is simple: every choice should feel like something a mid-century clerk would have done because it was correct procedure, and never like something a modern designer did because it looked charmingly old. If a detail exists only to be noticed, it has failed; if it exists to file the weather properly, it belongs.
